THE COMPLETE GUIDE TO PUPPY RAISING AND TRAINING

Raising a puppy is joyful, exhausting, and hugely formative. The experiences and training a puppy receives in its first months shape the adult dog it becomes, so this period rewards patience and consistency. This guide covers development, house training, socialisation, early manners, and the common challenges new puppy owners face.

Puppy development stages
Puppies grow through recognisable stages. In the first weeks with their litter they learn crucial lessons from their mother and siblings, which is why reputable breeders and rescues do not rehome puppies too early. Once home, usually from around eight weeks, a puppy enters a critical socialisation window that lasts until roughly sixteen weeks, during which positive experiences have an outsized effect on lifelong temperament. Adolescence, which can begin around six months, often brings a testing phase where previously learned behaviours seem to unravel; this is normal, and consistency carries you through it.

House training
House training relies on routine, supervision, and reward rather than punishment. Take the puppy outside frequently, especially after waking, eating, drinking, and playing, and reward it immediately when it eliminates outdoors so it links the location with good things. Puppies have limited bladder control and generally need to go out every couple of hours, so accidents are inevitable and should be cleaned thoroughly with an enzymatic cleaner to remove scent markers that would otherwise draw the puppy back. Never punish accidents, as this tends to teach the puppy to hide rather than to learn. Most puppies take several weeks to become reliably house-trained, and consistency shortens the process.

Crate training
A crate, introduced kindly, gives a puppy a safe den and supports both house training and calm settling. The crate should be big enough for the puppy to stand, turn, and lie down, and it should always be associated with good things such as treats, meals, and rest, never used as punishment. Introduce it gradually, letting the puppy enter voluntarily and building up time slowly so it never feels trapped. A well crate-trained dog will often choose to rest there, and crates make travel and veterinary visits far less stressful. Puppies should not be crated for long stretches, as they cannot hold their bladder long and need company.

Socialisation
Socialisation means positively exposing a puppy to a wide variety of people, animals, environments, sounds, and experiences so it grows into a confident, well-adjusted adult. The prime window is early puppyhood, up to about sixteen weeks, though socialisation continues throughout life. The key is that experiences are gentle and positive, never overwhelming, so the puppy builds good associations rather than fear. Poor socialisation is one of the leading causes of fear-based aggression and anxiety in adult dogs. Well-run puppy classes, calm introductions to new people and vaccinated dogs, and gradual exposure to everyday sights and sounds all help build a stable temperament.

Basic training and manners
Basic training builds communication and safety, and modern methods rely on positive reinforcement: rewarding wanted behaviour rather than punishing mistakes. Core cues include sit, stay, come, down, and loose-leash walking. Keep sessions short, frequent, and upbeat, ending on success, because puppies learn better in brief bursts than long drills. Consistency across everyone in the household matters, since mixed signals slow progress. Of all the skills, a reliable recall, coming when called, is the most safety-critical and deserves heavy investment: always make returning to you rewarding, and never call your puppy to you for something unpleasant.

Puppy biting and mouthing
Puppy biting and mouthing are completely normal parts of play and exploration, not signs of aggression, but they need gentle redirection. Puppies begin learning bite inhibition from their litter-mates, and you continue this by reacting to hard bites with a brief, calm pause in play, teaching that biting ends the fun. Redirect the puppy onto appropriate chew toys, and avoid games that encourage biting hands. Overtired puppies often bite more, so ensuring enough rest genuinely helps. With consistency, most puppies grow out of mouthing as they mature.

Chewing
Chewing is a natural and necessary behaviour, especially for teething puppies, so the goal is to redirect rather than eliminate it. Provide a range of appropriate chew toys and reward the puppy for using them, while puppy-proofing the environment to keep valuables and hazards out of reach. Destructive chewing that persists is often driven by boredom, anxiety, or excess energy, so meeting the puppy's exercise and enrichment needs usually helps. Punishing chewing after the fact does not work, because the puppy cannot connect the punishment to the earlier act.

Common mistakes to avoid
New owners commonly under-socialise during the critical window, expect too much too soon, or unintentionally reward unwanted behaviours by giving attention to them. Inconsistency between family members, using punishment that damages trust, and skipping mental stimulation in favour of only physical exercise are also frequent pitfalls. Perhaps the most important thing to remember is that raising a puppy is a marathon, not a sprint; calm, consistent, kind guidance over months produces a confident, well-mannered adult dog, while frustration and shortcuts tend to backfire. When problems feel overwhelming, a qualified force-free trainer or veterinary behaviourist is a valuable resource.
