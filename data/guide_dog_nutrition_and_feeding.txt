THE COMPLETE GUIDE TO DOG NUTRITION AND FEEDING

Good nutrition underpins a dog's health, energy, coat condition, and longevity. Feeding well is not complicated, but it does require choosing an appropriate diet, feeding the right amount, and avoiding harmful foods. This guide gives a general overview; your vet can advise on your individual dog's needs, particularly if it has a medical condition.

The basics of a balanced diet
Dogs need a complete and balanced diet that provides protein, fats, carbohydrates, vitamins, and minerals in appropriate proportions. Most owners meet this with a good-quality commercial dog food formulated to recognised nutritional standards for the dog's life stage. Complete foods are designed to provide everything a healthy dog needs without supplementation. Home-prepared and raw diets can be done, but they are easy to get wrong nutritionally and carry other risks, so they should only be undertaken with proper guidance from a veterinary nutritionist. Fresh, clean water should always be available.

Feeding for life stage
Nutritional needs change across a dog's life. Puppies grow rapidly and need diets formulated for growth, with the right balance of nutrients and, for large breeds, careful control to avoid growing too fast, which can harm developing joints. Adult dogs need a maintenance diet matched to their activity level. Senior dogs may benefit from diets adjusted for lower activity, weight management, or joint and organ support. Pregnant and nursing dogs have substantially higher needs. Choosing a food appropriate to your dog's current life stage is one of the simplest ways to support its health.

Portion control and obesity
Overfeeding is one of the most common mistakes owners make, and obesity is a widespread, preventable problem that shortens lifespan and worsens joint, heart, and metabolic conditions. Packet guidelines are a starting point, not a rule; they often overestimate, and the right amount depends on the individual dog's metabolism, activity, and body condition. The best guide is the dog's body itself: you should be able to feel the ribs easily and see a waist from above. Treats count towards daily calories and should make up only a small fraction of intake. Adjusting portions to maintain a lean body condition is one of the most valuable things an owner can do.

Feeding routine
Most adult dogs do well on two measured meals a day, which also supports house training and helps you notice appetite changes that can signal illness. Puppies need more frequent meals, often three or four a day when young, reducing as they grow. Sudden diet changes can upset the stomach, so transition to a new food gradually over several days by mixing increasing amounts of the new food with the old. For deep-chested breeds prone to bloat, avoiding vigorous exercise right after a large meal is a sensible precaution.

Treats and human food
Treats are useful for training and bonding but should be given in moderation and counted within the daily calorie budget. Many healthy options exist, including small pieces of dog-safe vegetables. However, a number of common human foods are toxic to dogs and must be avoided entirely: chocolate, xylitol (a sweetener in sugar-free products), grapes and raisins, onions, garlic, chives, macadamia nuts, alcohol, caffeine, and large amounts of fatty or salty food. Xylitol and grapes or raisins are dangerous even in small amounts. Cooked bones can splinter and cause serious internal injury and should never be given.

Special dietary needs
Some dogs have specific dietary needs due to allergies, sensitivities, or medical conditions such as kidney or liver disease, diabetes, or pancreatitis. Signs of food intolerance can include persistent digestive upset or skin problems, though these have many causes and warrant veterinary investigation rather than guesswork. Prescription and therapeutic diets exist for many conditions and should be used under veterinary direction. If you suspect your dog has a dietary problem, work with your vet rather than repeatedly switching foods on your own, which can make it harder to identify the cause.

Reading labels and choosing food
Choosing among the many available foods can be daunting. Look for foods that state they are complete and balanced for your dog's life stage according to a recognised standard, and consider the manufacturer's quality control and track record. Price is not a perfect guide to quality, and marketing terms on packaging are often unregulated. If you are unsure what to feed, your vet can give tailored recommendations based on your dog's age, breed, size, and health. The best diet is ultimately the one that keeps your individual dog healthy, lean, and thriving over time.
