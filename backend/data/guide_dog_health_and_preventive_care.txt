THE COMPLETE GUIDE TO DOG HEALTH AND PREVENTIVE CARE

Keeping a dog healthy is mostly about prevention: good routine care, watching for early signs of trouble, and knowing what to do in an emergency. This guide gives an overview of preventive health care and common concerns. It is general information and never a substitute for professional veterinary advice; your vet is your most important partner in your dog's health.

Routine veterinary care and vaccinations
Regular veterinary care protects dogs from serious preventable diseases and catches problems early. Core vaccines guard against illnesses such as parvovirus, distemper, and rabies, with the exact schedule and legal requirements varying by region, so follow your vet's guidance. Puppies receive a series of vaccinations in their first months, followed by periodic boosters through life. Annual or twice-yearly wellness checks let a vet monitor weight, teeth, and overall condition and update parasite prevention. Establishing a relationship with a vet before an emergency arises is strongly recommended.

Parasite prevention
Parasite control is a routine but essential part of care. External parasites such as fleas and ticks cause itching and can transmit disease, with ticks in particular spreading serious illnesses, so regular preventive treatment matters, especially in warmer months and grassy areas. Internal parasites such as roundworms and tapeworms require routine deworming, and in regions where heartworm occurs, specific preventives are vital. The right products and schedule depend on where the dog lives and its lifestyle, so let your vet guide you. Checking the coat after walks in tick-prone areas and removing ticks promptly reduces disease risk.

Dental care
Dental disease is one of the most common and most overlooked health problems in dogs, and it is largely preventable. Plaque and tartar build up into gum disease, causing pain, tooth loss, and bacteria that can affect the heart and kidneys. The gold standard is regular tooth brushing with dog-specific toothpaste, introduced gradually. Dental chews, appropriate diets, and professional cleanings under veterinary supervision also help. Small breeds with crowded teeth are especially prone and need extra attention. Bad breath is often the first sign of a developing problem and is worth a veterinary check rather than being ignored.

Weight and body condition
Obesity is one of the most common and preventable health problems in dogs, and it worsens nearly every other condition, from joint disease to heart and metabolic problems, while shortening lifespan. Feed a complete, balanced diet in measured portions appropriate to the dog's life stage, size, and activity, and adjust based on body condition rather than rigidly following packet guidelines. A healthy dog should have an easily felt ribcage and a visible waist. Regular exercise supports both physical and mental health. Keeping a dog lean is one of the most powerful things an owner can do for its long-term wellbeing.

Recognising illness and pain
Dogs instinctively hide weakness, so owners must watch for subtle changes. Warning signs of illness include loss of appetite, lethargy, vomiting or diarrhoea lasting more than a day, difficulty breathing, sudden behaviour changes, limping, and excessive drinking or urination. Signs of pain can be equally subtle: reluctance to jump or climb stairs, changes in posture or temperament, restlessness, excessive licking of one area, or panting unrelated to heat. Because these are easy to dismiss as ageing or mood, any persistent change deserves a veterinary check. Critically, never give a dog human painkillers such as ibuprofen or paracetamol, as they are toxic even in small doses; a vet can provide safe pain relief.

Recognising an emergency
Some situations are true emergencies where every minute counts. Seek immediate veterinary care for difficulty breathing, collapse or unconsciousness, a bloated hard abdomen with unproductive retching, seizures, suspected poisoning, severe bleeding, inability to urinate, suspected heatstroke, or major trauma. Bloat, in particular, is a rapidly fatal emergency most common in large, deep-chested breeds and must never be watched overnight. When unsure whether something is an emergency, call a vet or emergency clinic for advice rather than waiting. Knowing your nearest emergency clinic and its number in advance saves critical time.

Basic first aid and poisons
Basic first aid can stabilise a dog while you get it to a vet, but it never replaces veterinary care. Keep a simple kit with clean bandages, gauze, tape, and your vet's number. Many everyday substances are dangerous: foods including chocolate, xylitol, grapes and raisins, onions, and garlic; antifreeze, which is sweet-tasting and lethal in tiny amounts; many human medications; and certain plants and rodent poisons. If you suspect poisoning, contact a vet or an animal poison line immediately with details of what and how much was consumed, and do not wait for symptoms to appear.

Neutering, insurance, and lifelong care
Spaying and neutering prevent unwanted litters and can bring certain health and behavioural benefits, though the ideal timing depends on the individual dog and should be discussed with a vet. Because veterinary care can be expensive, planning ahead with pet insurance or a savings buffer is wise; read insurance terms carefully, as coverage and exclusions vary. As dogs age, they need more frequent checks and may develop arthritis, dental disease, or organ changes, many of which are manageable when caught early. Preventive care, attentive observation, and a good relationship with your vet give a dog the best chance of a long, comfortable life.
