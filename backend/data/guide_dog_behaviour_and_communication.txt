THE COMPLETE GUIDE TO DOG BEHAVIOUR AND COMMUNICATION

Understanding how dogs communicate and why they behave as they do makes you a better, kinder owner and prevents many problems. Most unwanted behaviour is normal dog behaviour that is inconvenient to humans, or a response to unmet needs, fear, or confusion, rather than stubbornness or spite. This guide covers body language, the philosophy of modern training, and the common behaviour problems owners encounter.

Reading canine body language
Dogs communicate constantly through body language, and learning to read it protects both dogs and people. A relaxed dog has a loose body, soft eyes, and a naturally wagging tail. Stress shows through a tucked tail, flattened ears, lip-licking, yawning out of context, a tense posture, or showing the whites of the eyes. A common and dangerous misconception is that any wagging tail means friendliness; a high, stiff wag can signal arousal or threat. Signs that a dog wants space include turning away, freezing, or backing off, and ignoring these can lead to bites. Teaching children to recognise these signals and to give dogs space is an essential safety measure.

The philosophy of modern training
Modern, evidence-based training rests on positive reinforcement: rewarding the behaviour you want so the dog is more likely to repeat it. This approach builds confidence and a strong bond, whereas punishment-based and dominance-based methods can create fear and aggression and damage trust. The outdated idea that owners must be a dominant "pack leader" has been largely discredited by behavioural science; dogs are not trying to take over the household. Effective training instead depends on clear communication, consistency, good timing, and meeting the dog's needs. When a dog does something we dislike, the useful question is what need or trigger drives the behaviour, and how we can redirect it.

Why behaviour problems happen
Many behaviour problems trace back to unmet physical or mental needs, insufficient socialisation, fear, or inadvertent reinforcement by owners. A bored, under-exercised dog will find its own often destructive outlets. A poorly socialised dog may react fearfully to normal things. And behaviours we dislike are frequently rewarded without our realising, as when jumping up earns attention or barking makes a visitor "go away." Understanding the cause is the first step to solving the problem, because suppressing a symptom without addressing its driver rarely works and can make things worse.

Common behaviour problems
Excessive barking usually has an identifiable cause such as boredom, alarm, attention-seeking, or anxiety, and the right response depends on which it is. Jumping up is best resolved by removing the reward of attention and rewarding a calm alternative such as sitting. Destructive chewing in adult dogs is often boredom or anxiety and improves with more exercise and enrichment. Pulling on the leash is addressed by ensuring pulling never gets the dog where it wants to go while rewarding a loose leash. Resource guarding, where a dog protects food or objects, is a natural instinct; a growl is valuable communication that should never be punished, and mild cases improve by teaching the dog that people approaching its resources predicts good things.

Fear, anxiety, and separation
Fear-based behaviours, including noise phobias around thunderstorms or fireworks, are common and should be handled with patience rather than force. Comforting a frightened dog does not reinforce fear, so providing reassurance and a safe hiding place is appropriate, while forcing a dog to confront what scares it tends to make the phobia worse. Separation anxiety, genuine distress when left alone, shows as destruction focused on exits, excessive vocalising, or house-soiling soon after departure, and it is different from boredom. Management focuses on gradually building tolerance to being alone, providing enrichment, and keeping departures and arrivals low-key. Serious fear or separation problems benefit from professional help.

Enrichment and meeting needs
Much problem behaviour is prevented simply by meeting a dog's needs well. Beyond physical exercise, dogs need mental stimulation through sniffing, foraging, problem-solving, and training, and a mentally satisfied dog is generally calmer. Enrichment options include puzzle feeders, snuffle mats, scatter-feeding, food-stuffed toys, and allowing plenty of sniffing on walks. Matching activity to the individual dog's breed and energy level matters, since a high-drive working breed needs far more than a laid-back companion breed. Meeting these needs is not a luxury but a foundation of good behaviour.

When to seek professional help
While many issues can be improved with knowledge and consistency, some warrant professional guidance, particularly anything involving aggression, severe fear or anxiety, or behaviour that is escalating or that you do not feel equipped to handle safely. Look for qualified, force-free trainers or, for more serious cases, veterinary behaviourists, and be wary of anyone promising instant results through harsh methods. It is also worth remembering that sudden behaviour changes can have medical causes, so a veterinary check is a sensible first step when a dog's behaviour shifts unexpectedly. Good behaviour ultimately grows from understanding, patience, and a relationship built on trust.
